Wildfire Impacts and Fire Danger

Fire-spread simulation · operational danger assessment · prescribed-fire planning

Wildfire danger emerges from the interaction of weather, fuels, terrain, and management history. Our research turns those interacting controls into spatially explicit estimates of fire growth and management-relevant risk. Two 2026 studies show this work at complementary scales: one builds a daily forecasting framework for the Southern Great Plains, while the other asks how often prescribed fire is needed to keep tallgrass prairie wildfires manageable.

Featured Research

Jia Yang · International Journal of Wildland Fire · 2026

Wildfire Danger Assessment and Forecast Through Fire Spread Simulations

Jia Yang et al. · Volume 35, Issue 8 · DOI: 10.1071/WF25278

This study introduces the Wildfire Danger Assessment and Forecast (WDAF) framework, a physically grounded alternative to relying on weather indices alone. WDAF combines daily weather, spatial fuels, and topography in FARSITE, then expresses danger through simulated fire size and its percentile within a historical reference distribution.

  • Regional design: the framework was implemented across 356 hexagonal analysis units in the Southern Great Plains using a 2016–2022 historical reference period.
  • Operational metric: simulated daily fire size captures potential spread, while the historical percentile makes danger comparable through time and across locations.
  • Evaluation: comparison with observed large-fire activity demonstrates how simulation-derived danger can support spatial forecasting and preparedness.

Alex Desjardins · Fire Ecology · 2026

Model-based Assessment of Prescribed Burning Frequency to Mitigate Wildfire Risks in Tallgrass Prairie

Alex Desjardins et al. · Published 1 July 2026 · DOI: 10.1186/s42408-026-00538-9

Field measurements from the Tallgrass Prairie Preserve were combined with Monte Carlo fire-spread modeling to test how quickly the risk-reduction benefits of prescribed burning diminish as fuels recover. The experiment crossed seven post-burn fuel conditions—from 1 to 40 growing-season months—with seven Burning Index weather categories.

  • Rapid fuel recovery: grass fuel loads exceeded 3 metric tons per hectare by ten growing-season months after burning.
  • Early return of intense behavior: at five months, modeled flame lengths exceeded 1.2 m in every weather category and 2.4 m under extreme conditions.
  • Management implication: productive tallgrass prairie may require frequent prescribed fire to maintain a meaningful wildfire-mitigation window.

Shared Research Approach

  • Process-based fire behavior simulation across realistic combinations of weather, fuel, and terrain.
  • Field and geospatial data used to parameterize, evaluate, and interpret model behavior.
  • Translation of model output into comparable danger classes and practical management thresholds.
