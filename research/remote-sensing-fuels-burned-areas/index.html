Satellite Remote Sensing and Geospatial Modeling

Geostationary satellite observations · hourly fire progression · environmental drivers

Geostationary satellites make it possible to observe wildfire growth at the time scale at which fire behavior changes. We use these observations with weather, fuels, and terrain data to characterize when large fires accelerate, where rapid-growth events cluster, and which environmental conditions are most strongly associated with hourly progression.

Featured Research

Shanmin Fang · International Journal of Applied Earth Observation and Geoinformation · 2026

Satellite-based analysis of hourly progression and driving factors of large U.S. wildfires

Shanmin Fang et al. · Volume 149, Article 105288 · DOI: 10.1016/j.jag.2026.105288

The study reconstructs the hourly progression of 294 large wildfires observed by GOES-16 between June 2017 and December 2024. These satellite-derived trajectories are analyzed with weather and landscape variables to build a consistent national view of rapid fire growth.

  • Geographic hotspots: the Central Great Plains and Northern California contain prominent clusters of fast hourly progression.
  • Afternoon acceleration: fire growth generally peaks during the afternoon, aligning with the daily cycle of atmospheric and fuel conditions.
  • Exceptional events: the Smokehouse Creek Fire reached the highest observed hourly progression rate, approximately 320 km² per hour.
  • Dominant control: wind is the environmental factor most strongly associated with rapid hourly growth.

Research Contribution

The resulting event-level dataset provides a benchmark for evaluating fire-spread models, testing high-frequency danger products, and studying the short windows in which extreme wildfire growth occurs. The workflow also demonstrates how geostationary observations can complement daily burned-area products and incident reports.

Research Approach

  • High-temporal-resolution fire detection from geostationary satellite data.
  • Event reconstruction and quality control for large, multi-day wildfires.
  • Spatial and temporal attribution using weather and landscape variables.
